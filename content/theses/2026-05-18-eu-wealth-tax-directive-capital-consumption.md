# The 2026 EU Wealth-Tax Directive: Capital-Consumption Analysis

The European Parliament approved on May 15, 2026 a directive requiring all member states to enact progressive wealth taxes of 1–3 percent on net assets above €1 million by 2028, rising to 5 percent above €50 million. Rapporteur Marta Sánchez said the directive "finally makes wealth pay its fair share" and described it as "a historic correction of decades of inequality and the moral foundation of a just Europe"; critics' capital-flight warnings were dismissed as "right-wing fearmongering driven by oligarch interests, not real economics." This thesis rejects the framing — not the description of the statute, which is accurate, but the categorical claims that wealth "pay[s]" a tax and that the warning the rapporteur dismissed is not economics.

## The Tax Falls on the Stock, Not the Flow

[[rothbard-on-wealth-tax|Rothbard]] names what a wealth tax is in [[power-and-market|Power and Market]] Ch. 4 §C. It is not a property tax (which attaches to a specific asset and is *capitalized* into its market price, cushioning the initial blow). It is not an income tax (which falls on the flow). It is a tax on the *person* equal to a percentage of net worth, and "could not be capitalized, since the tax would not be attached to a property, where it could be discounted by the market." Rothbard's structural conclusion: "the wealth tax levies a heavy penalty on accumulated wealth and that therefore the effect of the tax will be to slash accumulated capital. No quicker route could be found to promote capital consumption and general impoverishment than to penalize the accumulation of capital."

The numeric design of the directive maps onto Rothbard's mechanism with unusual precision. A 5 percent annual rate on net wealth above €50 million is not, under normal pre-tax interest rates, payable from the wealth's income alone. Payment requires liquidating principal — the literal "slash[ing of] accumulated capital" the passage names. The 1–3 percent band above €1 million is closer to typical net real yields and so its first-order effect is closer to the flow-tax mechanism Rothbard treats separately; but even there the *cumulative* mechanism Rothbard identifies in [[sales-tax-incidence|Sales Tax Incidence]]'s sister analysis applies — the tax falls back on original-factor incomes, not on "wealth" as a free-floating object.

## "Wealth" Does Not Pay

The rapporteur's framing — the directive "finally makes wealth pay its fair share" — treats "wealth" as a taxpayer. The wiki's tax-incidence frame rejects that grammar at the level of the predicate. Wealth is held by persons. Taxes are paid by persons. Rothbard's general no-forward-shifting claim from [[sales-tax-incidence|Sales Tax Incidence]] means the burden of any tax on wealth falls — through liquidation, through reduced savings-investment flow, through suppressed wages where capital is the complementary factor — on real persons working in productive sectors of the European economy. Hoppe makes the corresponding general claim in [[the-economics-and-ethics-of-private-property|The Economics and Ethics of Private Property]]: no amount of any tax can be shifted onto "consumers" or onto "wealth" as such, because production is touched in every case. The "fair share" frame asserts that an abstract holding class pays; the wiki's frame names which actual incomes are reduced.

## Capital Consumption as the Diagnostic Frame

[[mises-on-capital-consumption|Mises]] gave this kind of policy its standing name in [[liberalism|Liberalism]] Part I §5: "Antiliberal policy is a policy of capital consumption. It recommends that the present be more abundantly provided for at the expense of the future." The directive's design — a present revenue stream produced by penalising the accumulation that would have funded future production — is precisely the structure Mises names. The rapporteur calls this "a historic correction of decades of inequality"; the wiki names it as the recurring rhetorical move Mises specifically diagnosed in the same chapter, where the antiliberal "denounces [the liberal] as a hard-hearted enemy of the people" while "setting himself up as a friend of humanity." The voice is dated; the structure is the same one operative in May 2026.

## The Caretaker's Capital-Consumption Incentive

Hoppe's [[democracy-the-god-that-failed|Democracy: The God That Failed]] supplies the regime-mechanism: "a prince tends to avoid increasing taxes if this leads to capital consumption — a drop in the present discounted value of the capital stock of which he is the owner. In contrast, a caretaker shows no such reluctance. … He only owns the present tax-revenue, he does not own the capital stock from which it is derived — others do." A directive passed by a five-year-cycle democratic body, imposing a tax whose mechanism is capital consumption, on a wealth base it does not own, is the textbook case Hoppe's chapter predicts. The wiki does not need the rapporteur to be cynical or corrupt for the prediction to apply; the prediction is structural. Caretakers are indifferent to capital consumption because they do not own future tax revenue from the capital stock.

The Hoppe citation has a scope qualification the wiki should be honest about. Hoppe's *general* claim is that princely rule moderates this incentive better than democratic rule; he does not infer that monarchy is desirable. The wiki uses Hoppe's mechanism, not his political ranking, here.

## Political Means at Supra-State Scale

The Oppenheimer–Nock distinction underwrites the classification. [[political-means-and-economic-means|Political Means and Economic Means]] separates production-and-voluntary-exchange (the economic means) from coercive appropriation (the political means). A directive that compels twenty-seven member states to enact a tax on accumulated net assets is political means by definition, and at a scale — supra-state coordination removing the exit option through inter-state tax competition — that intensifies the mechanism. Nock's [[our-enemy-the-state|Our Enemy, the State]] names the larger pattern as the state-power-absorbing-social-power process: "social power" in Nock's sense is precisely the accumulated capital of voluntary production-and-exchange, which "state power" converts into administrative revenue and political program.

## The Dismissal That Names the Frame

The most informative line in the news is the rapporteur's: capital-flight warnings are "right-wing fearmongering driven by oligarch interests, not real economics." The structural-economics literature this wiki documents does not require any "right-wing" priors to predict capital flight. It is the direct consequence of [[rothbard-on-wealth-tax|Rothbard's]] point that a wealth tax cannot be capitalized into asset prices and follows the *person*: the person's response is migration to lower-tax jurisdictions where the tax does not follow. The 2017 French repeal of the *impôt de solidarité sur la fortune* (after net-worth tax revenue had been counterweighted by exit-driven base erosion) is a recent empirical case the wiki has not yet ingested, but the structural argument does not need it: the prediction follows from the no-capitalization property alone. The dismissal as "not real economics" inverts the wiki's frame — what the rapporteur calls *not* economics is precisely the prediction the structural literature makes; what she calls "real economics" is the political program. That inversion is the rhetorical structure [[mises-on-capital-consumption|Mises]] named.

## Scope Qualifications

Three qualifications belong in the wiki's voice. First, the wiki's prediction is *structural*, not date-stamped. It does not say revenue will undershoot the directive's projections in any specific year; it says the capital stock will fall relative to the no-directive counterfactual, that the burden will fall on original-factor incomes rather than on "wealth," and that exit pressure will erode the base. The empirical magnitudes depend on enforcement, asset valuation rules, exit-tax design, and pre-existing tax-treaty obligations the directive's text does not yet resolve. Second, the [[liberalism|Liberalism]] capital-consumption passage is consequentialist; the [[the-ethics-of-liberty|natural-rights]] critique Rothbard gives elsewhere is stronger but distinct, and the wiki's critique here rests on the consequentialist branch — the directive's *stated* moral foundation is welfare-of-Europeans, and on that ground the capital-consumption diagnosis is the direct counter-argument. Third, the wiki does not infer from the critique that *no* state revenue from existing assets is permissible; that is the libertarian conclusion in Rothbard's [[the-ethics-of-liberty|The Ethics of Liberty]] and Hoppe's [[a-theory-of-socialism-and-capitalism|A Theory of Socialism and Capitalism]], but the present thesis stops at rejecting the directive's specific framing.

## What the Wiki's Frame Does Not Cover

The wiki has no focused article on the post-1990 European wealth-tax repeal sequence (Austria 1994, Denmark 1997, Germany 1997, the Netherlands 2001, Finland 2006, Sweden 2007, France 2017) or on the OECD's 2018 analytic review of why most member states retreated from net-worth taxation. Those would supply the empirical-historical layer the structural argument here implies but does not contain. The wiki also has no focused article on Hoppe's caretaker-capital-consumption ranking from [[democracy-the-god-that-failed|Democracy: The God That Failed]] Ch. IV; the claim is presently available only through the broad reference page, and a focused `hoppe-on-caretaker-capital-consumption` article would tighten the citation.
