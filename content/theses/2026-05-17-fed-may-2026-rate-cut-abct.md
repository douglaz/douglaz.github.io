# The May 2026 Fed Rate Cut: ABCT Analysis

The Federal Reserve cut interest rates by 25 basis points in May 2026 — its third cut of the year — and the news reports the immediate sectoral response: "Mortgage rates fell to 6.1% the following week and home sales rose 4% in the same period." This is the textbook signature [[austrian-business-cycle-theory|Austrian Business Cycle Theory]] names. The wiki's frame predicts it.

## The Mechanism the News Instantiates

[[mises-on-credit-expansion|Mises]] treats credit expansion as the issuance of additional fiduciary media offered on the loan market, which drops the gross market rate of interest below the level voluntary saving and time preference would equilibrate. In [[human-action|Human Action]] Ch. XX, the calculational consequence is direct: "the drop in interest rates falsifies the businessman's calculation… They make some projects appear profitable and realizable which a correct calculation, based on an interest rate not manipulated by credit expansion, would have shown as unrealizable" (Scholar's Edition pp. 550–551). What is being measured in May 2026 is the front edge of that falsification: a one-week pass-through from the policy rate to mortgage rates of roughly the cut's magnitude, and a 4% sales response in the most interest-rate-sensitive sector of the consumer economy.

[[rothbard-on-fed-induced-boom|Rothbard]] supplies the sectoral prediction. In [[americas-great-depression|America's Great Depression]] Ch. 1, capital-goods industries — especially "construction, and equipment" — "expand much further in the boom, and are hit far more severely in the depression" (p. 9). Residential construction is the largest single component of that pattern in the modern American economy. The thesis's positive prediction is not that the cut will fail to stimulate housing; it is that the stimulus is what gives malinvestment its concrete content. The boom is the misallocation, not its absence.

## The Framing the Wiki Rejects

The post quotes the Fed's stated rationale: "cooling labor markets." That is the Phillips-curve framing the wiki's monetary tradition rejects. The relevant question is not whether labor-market slack justifies a rate cut on aggregate-demand grounds; it is what the cut *does* to the structure of production. [[austrian-business-cycle-theory|ABCT]]'s critique is not that central banks have the wrong reaction function — it is that the institution of central-bank rate-setting is the source of the cycle. The rate the Fed sets is not a market rate; it is the price at which newly created [[credit-and-deferred-payment|circulation credit]] is offered on the loan market. Lowering it suppresses the calculational signal that would otherwise prevent entrepreneurs from lengthening the production structure beyond what voluntary savings can sustain.

## Scope Qualifications

Two qualifications belong in the wiki's voice rather than the slogans. First, [[mises-on-credit-expansion|Mises]] draws the credit-expansion/no-expansion line at fiduciary-media issuance, not at policy-rate movements as such: a cut that simply ratifies a fall in time preference is not destructive. The wiki article on [[credit-and-deferred-payment|Credit and Deferred Payment]] flags this distinction explicitly. The third cut of a single calendar year, in the absence of any reason to think voluntary saving has risen, is not such a benign case.

Second, [[rothbard-on-fed-induced-boom|Rothbard]] frames the predicted trajectory as a *cluster* of errors visible only in retrospect — not as a date-stamped forecast of a particular liquidation event. The thesis's claim is structural: housing demand stimulated by suppressed interest rates is exactly the kind of demand the theory says will be revealed as malinvestment when rates adjust. It is not a prediction that the bust arrives next quarter.

## What the Wiki's Frame Does Not Cover

The wiki has no focused article on the modern transmission channel from the Fed funds rate to thirty-year mortgage rates (the mortgage-backed-securities market, agency duration risk, the Treasury yield curve as intermediate signal). [[americas-great-depression|America's Great Depression]] treats the 1920s commercial-paper and bank-credit channels; the modern housing-finance plumbing is downstream of that frame but not in the corpus. The structural ABCT claim does not require it, but a richer reading would.
