# Mises on Capital Consumption

> [[liberalism|Mises]] treats the question of taxation, redistribution, and the various Sozialpolitik proposals of his day as instances of a single technical category, not as separate welfare debates. The category is *capital consumption*: an antiliberal policy is one that increases present consumption at the cost of the capital stock that would otherwise produce future income. The passage refuses the moral framing the policies are usually defended under and substitutes a praxeological one.

## The Passage

> "Antiliberal policy is a policy of capital consumption. It recommends that the present be more abundantly provided for at the expense of the future. It is in exactly the same case as the patient of whom we have spoken. In both instances a relatively grievous disadvantage in the future stands in opposition to a relatively abundant momentary gratification. To talk, in such a case, as if the question were one of hard-heartedness versus philanthropy is downright dishonest and untruthful."

Mises is explicit elsewhere in [[liberalism|Liberalism]] that the operative mechanism runs through the institution of private property: *"the institution of private property is curbed and hindered in every quarter today by antiliberal policies."* A measure that takes from the existing stock of capital is, on this view, structurally different from one that taxes a flow of new production — and structurally worse, because the stock is what produces every future flow.

## Why the Naming Matters

The passage works at the level of category, not slogan. A specific policy belongs to the antiliberal class — and so to the capital-consumption class — when its incidence falls on accumulated capital rather than on current consumption. The popular framing under which such policies are defended ("fair share," "moral correction," "fearmongering by oligarchs") is exactly the framing Mises calls "downright dishonest" — not because the motivations are insincere, but because the framing systematically conceals the intertemporal trade. The patient analogy is the controlling image: a treatment that relieves present discomfort by withdrawing from the body's reserves is not "philanthropy" simply because it delivers present relief.

## Relation to Rothbard

Rothbard supplies the microeconomic derivation in [[rothbard-on-wealth-tax|Rothbard on the Wealth Tax]]: a tax denominated in net worth cannot be capitalized into asset prices and cannot be shifted forward in price, so its incidence falls directly on accumulated capital. Mises's "antiliberal policy is a policy of capital consumption" is the categorical statement; Rothbard's [[power-and-market|Power and Market]] Ch. 4 §4.C is the worked-out mechanism for the wealth-tax instance.

## Scope

The wiki's [[liberalism|Liberalism]] preserves a minarchist carve-out for a strictly limited protective state and treats *that* state as legitimate rather than parasitic. The carve-out covers protection of person, property, and contract — it does not cover confiscatory taxes on accumulated wealth, which fall under the antiliberal category by the exact mechanism the passage names. [[road-to-serfdom|Hayek]] takes a parallel position from a rule-of-law angle.
