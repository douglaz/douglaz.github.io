# Rothbard on Fed-Induced Booms

> "In the purely free and unhampered market, there will be no cluster of errors, since trained entrepreneurs will not all make errors at the same time. The 'boom-bust' cycle is generated by monetary intervention in the market, specifically bank credit expansion to business. … Now what happens when banks print new money (whether as bank notes or bank deposits) and lend it to business? The new money pours forth on the loan market and lowers the loan rate of interest. It looks as if the supply of saved funds for investment has increased, for the effect is the same: the supply of funds for invest­ment apparently increases, and the interest rate is lowered. Businessmen, in short, are misled by the bank inflation into believing that the supply of saved funds is greater than it really is."

[[americas-great-depression|Rothbard]] treats the *cluster of errors* — the simultaneous failure of many trained entrepreneurs whose individual judgments would ordinarily not move in lockstep — as the diagnostic that distinguishes ABCT from rival depression theories. A free market cannot produce that pattern; only a common monetary signal aimed at all entrepreneurs at once can. The Federal Reserve is the institutional source of that signal in the post-1913 American economy, and Rothbard's introduction to the 5th edition is unambiguous about the application: *"the monetary inflation of the 1920s set the stage inevitably for the depression, a depression which was further aggravated (and unsound investments maintained) by the Federal Reserve efforts to inflate further during the 1930s."*

The mechanism the boom rides is the lengthening of the structure of production financed by funds that do not represent voluntary saving. Capital-goods industries — especially construction, raw materials, and equipment — expand most violently in the boom and contract most violently in the bust, because their profitability depends most heavily on the lower-than-warranted interest rate. Housing finance sits inside this Rothbardian description by construction: mortgages are credit at long horizons collateralized by capital goods that are highly interest-rate-sensitive. When the rate falls because the Fed has supplied new credit rather than because households have saved more, the resulting housing activity is the canonical malinvestment the cycle theory predicts.
