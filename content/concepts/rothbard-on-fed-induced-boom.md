# Rothbard on Fed-Induced Booms

> "No businessman in the real world is equipped with perfect foresight; all make errors. But the free-market process precisely rewards those businessmen who are equipped to make a minimum number of errors. Why should there suddenly be a cluster of errors? Furthermore, why should these errors particularly pervade the capital-goods industries?"
> — [[man-economy-and-state|Rothbard, Man, Economy, and State]], Ch. 12 §8

Rothbard's diagnostic for distinguishing a market-driven shift in investment from a credit-induced one is the *cluster of errors*. Entrepreneurs make individual mistakes constantly; what requires explanation is the synchronized over-investment of an entire stratum of the economy — the higher-order, capital-intensive, long-horizon stages of production. In [[americas-great-depression|America's Great Depression]] Rothbard names the empirical regularity directly: "The capital-goods industries — especially the industries supplying raw materials, construction, and equipment to other industries — expand much further in the boom, and are hit far more severely in the depression."

Rothbard's institutional point is that the Federal Reserve is the source of the synchronization. A central bank's lowering of the policy rate (or its bond purchases, reserve-requirement cuts, or other forms of fiat-money creation) feeds new fiduciary media into the loan market. The gross market rate of interest drops below the rate that voluntary saving and time preference would sustain. Long-horizon, interest-rate-sensitive sectors — construction, residential real estate, durable capital goods — light up first and hardest, because their present values are most sensitive to the discount rate. "Businessmen were misled by bank credit inflation to invest too much in higher-order capital goods, which could only be prosperously sustained through lower time preferences and greater savings and investment" ([[americas-great-depression|America's Great Depression]], p. 12). The historical claim Rothbard makes for the 1920s — Fed credit expansion under Benjamin Strong produced the structural distortions that made 1929 inevitable — is the policy-specific application of this mechanism.
